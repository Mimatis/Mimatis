Support in Germany

Verified support and information

We are so sorry that you are here. Whether your baby died early in pregnancy, later in pregnancy, at birth or soon after, your baby matters and your grief is real. In Germany, babies who die before or around birth are often called Sternenkinder — star children. A birth after your baby has died is often called a stille Geburt, a silent birth.

This page gathers what parents in Germany need to know, in the order you are likely to need it. Please take only what you need today. The rest will still be here.

MIMATIS shares information and trusted links. It is not legal or medical advice. Rules can change, and burial rules differ between the 16 federal states (Bundesländer). Please confirm anything important with your hospital team, the registry office (Standesamt), your health insurer or your employer. Information checked in October 2026.

If you are in danger, or thinking about ending your life, please call 112 now. The Telefonseelsorge listens day and night, free and confidentially: 0800 111 0 111, 0800 111 0 222 or 116 123.

The most important thing to know: 500 grams and week 24

In Germany, the law draws a line for paperwork. A baby who shows no sign of life after birth counts as stillborn (Totgeburt) if the baby weighed 500 grams or more, or if pregnancy had reached week 24. Either one is enough. Otherwise, it is called a Fehlgeburt, a miscarriage. A baby who shows any sign of life after birth, such as a heartbeat or breathing, counts as born alive, whatever the weight or week.

What happens in your situation

Early miscarriage

Losing a baby in the first months of pregnancy is very common, and it is still a real loss. Many parents feel alone with it, because others may not have known about the pregnancy. Your grief deserves care and time.

  • Your doctor will talk with you about your options. Depending on your situation, these can include waiting for the miscarriage to happen naturally, medicine, or a short procedure. Please ask what each one involves.
  • You can ask for an ultrasound print as a last image.
  • If you would like, you can still report your baby at the registry office and ask for a certificate. See registering your baby.
  • If you need time to recover, your doctor can give you sick leave (Krankschreibung). You do not have to tell your employer why. See time off work.
  • From a loss after week 12, you are protected from dismissal for four months. From week 13, you also have maternity protection.
  • Many families mark an early loss in their own way: lighting a candle, planting a tree, writing a letter, keeping the ultrasound image.

Later loss, under 500 grams and before week 24

When a baby dies in the second trimester, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. This can be a shock. Please know that you can ask to see and hold your baby, and to create memories, just as after a later loss.

  • You can spend time with your baby, give your baby a name and take photos. See at the hospital.
  • You can report your baby at the registry office and ask for a certificate. See registering your baby.
  • You have the right to bury your baby in every state. If you do not arrange a funeral, the hospital usually arranges one, often in a shared grave. If you would like your own farewell, tell the hospital team as early as you can. See saying goodbye.
  • Maternity protection depends on the week of pregnancy: 2 weeks from week 13, 6 weeks from week 17, 8 weeks from week 20, with maternity benefit. See time off work.
  • Your health insurance covers midwife visits at home. See when you go home.

Stillbirth, 500 grams or more, or week 24 or later

When a baby who weighed 500 grams or more, or who reached week 24, dies before or during birth, German law treats it as a birth in several important ways.

  • The birth is registered. Your baby is entered in the birth register as stillborn, and you can have your baby’s first name entered. See registering your baby.
  • You have the full maternity protection after birth (Mutterschutz), 8 weeks, with maternity benefit (Mutterschaftsgeld). See time off work.
  • A funeral is required in many states, and you have the right to bury your baby in all of them. See saying goodbye.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby lived, even for a very short time, your baby is registered as a birth and as a death. The mother has maternity protection: 8 weeks, or 12 weeks after a premature or multiple birth. The law allows a mother to go back to work after the first two weeks if she asks and her doctor agrees. She can change her mind at any time.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Unless there is a medical emergency, you do not need to rush. If you learn that your baby has died before birth, you can talk with the team about the timing of the birth and about pain relief. Some parents go home for a short time first, to gather the people they need.

These are things you can ask for:

  • To see and hold your baby, for as long as you need. You decide when to hand your baby to the midwife.
  • For family to meet your baby, including brothers and sisters. Children often want to see the baby.
  • A blessing or an emergency baptism (Nottaufe), through the hospital chaplaincy (Klinikseelsorge), if this matters to you.
  • Someone to describe your baby to you, if you are not ready to look yourself.
  • To understand why. Ask the team which tests are possible and what they involve. Take the time to decide.

You do not have to do any of this. Many parents later say that time with their baby helped them in their grief. Whatever you choose is right for you.

Creating memories

You can ask the team for:

  • Photos — on your own phone, or taken by the staff
  • Hand and foot prints
  • A lock of hair
  • The cloth your baby was wrapped in
  • A memory box, if the hospital has them

Free professional photos: the Dein Sternenkind foundation sends volunteer photographers to families who have lost a baby, free of charge. This covers babies who die during pregnancy from about week 14, at birth or soon after. You can ask for a photographer online or by phone on +49 9406 95 88 755. Ask your midwife or the hospital team too.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Hospital practice varies across Germany. If you feel unheard, you can ask to speak with the midwife in charge, the hospital chaplaincy or a hospital psychologist. Ask whether the hospital has a patient advocate (Patientenfürsprecher). A support organisation near you can also help.

Registering your baby and giving a name

German law gives every parent a way to have their baby officially recorded, whatever your baby weighed.

Under 500 grams and before week 24
  • Registration is not required, but you can report your baby to the registry office (Standesamt) of the place where the loss happened. A parent who would have had custody (Personensorge) if your baby had been born alive can report.
  • On request, the registry office gives you a certificate (Bescheinigung).
  • The Bundestag’s research service notes that your baby can be given an official name this way. Registry offices handle this in different ways, so please ask yours how the name is written on the certificate.
  • We found no deadline in the law for this report. Ask your registry office if you are reporting a loss from the past.
500 grams or more, or week 24 or later
  • The stillbirth must be reported to the registry office. The law sets the limit at three working days after the birth. The hospital usually does this. Please check with the team.
  • Your baby is entered in the birth register with the note that your baby was stillborn.
  • Your baby’s first name is entered if you ask for it. Please tell the hospital team and the registry office that you want this.
  • Ask for the confirmation and the certificates you need for your health insurer and your employer.

If your baby was born alive and then died, your baby is registered as a birth and as a death.

Saying goodbye: funerals

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way, at any stage of pregnancy. Funeral law is made by each of the 16 states.

  • Under 500 grams (Fehlgeburt): since 2013, you have the right to bury your baby in every state, if you wish.
  • Stillbirth: burial is required in many states from 500 grams, and in some states only from 1,000 grams. In every state you have the right to bury your baby.
  • If you do not arrange a funeral, the hospital usually makes sure your baby is laid to rest, often in a shared grave (Sammelgrab). This often costs you nothing.
  • Rules differ on whether the hospital must tell you about your options, and we cannot promise that yours will. Please ask the team, and say what you want, as early as you can.
Your options
  • A shared grave for babies, arranged by the hospital. Ask the cemetery office (Friedhofsverwaltung) or the hospital chaplaincy about dates, visiting and memorial services.
  • A grave of your own, arranged with a funeral home (Bestattungsunternehmen). Costs vary, so ask for a written quote. You can search for one near you at the Bundesverband Deutscher Bestatter and ask about baby funerals.
  • Your own ceremony: music, readings, carrying the coffin yourselves, or a last look at your baby, agreed with the funeral home. A religious funeral is possible too.
Places of memory

Many cemeteries and hospitals in Germany have a grave or a memorial service for Sternenkinder. Hospital chaplaincies and parishes can tell you what exists near you. Every year on the second Sunday of December at 19:00, families around the world light a candle for their children (Worldwide Candle Lighting).

Time off work and money

You do not have to go back to work while you are in shock. Here is what German law offers at the moment. It applies to employees. If you are self-employed, the maternity protection law does not apply to you, so please ask your health insurer what you are entitled to. Civil servants keep their full salary during protection periods.

  • The new rules for a miscarriage from week 13 have applied since 1 June 2025.
  • After a stillbirth, you can go back to work earlier if you ask for it and your doctor sees no problem. The earliest is the third week after the birth. You can change your mind at any time.
  • Proof for your employer and insurer: from 1 January 2026, doctors use a form called “Bescheinigung einer Fehlgeburt, Frühgeburt oder Behinderung des Kindes”. It can also serve as proof for the maternity benefit.
  • If you had not told your employer about the pregnancy, you decide whether to tell them about the loss. If you do not, the protection cannot apply, and you work unless you are on sick leave.
  • Maternity benefit (Mutterschaftsgeld) is paid by your statutory health insurer. Your employer adds a top-up. If you do not have a statutory insurer, you apply to the Bundesamt für Soziale Sicherung.
  • For partners: we did not find a statutory leave for partners after the death of a baby. Your contract or collective agreement (Tarifvertrag) may give days off, so please ask your employer, and your own doctor can give you sick leave if you cannot work.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • A midwife at home: statutory health insurance covers midwife visits at home after a stillbirth and after a miscarriage. After a miscarriage, you need a doctor’s referral. If you have private insurance, ask your insurer. She checks how your body is healing and can support you in your grief. You can search for one on ammely.de, the platform of the German Midwives’ Association, or ask the hospital.
  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in. Ask your midwife about your options before you leave hospital.
  • A check-up with your gynaecologist after a few weeks is a good idea. You can ask any question there.
  • Free counselling: the counselling centres of pro familia help with pregnancy, birth and family questions. Their search finds a centre by postcode. Ask whether they support parents after a loss.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Talking together about how each of you feels can help.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words and including them in remembering usually help. Children and teenagers can call the Kinder- und Jugendtelefon on 116 111, free, Monday to Saturday 14:00–20:00. Parents can call the Elterntelefon on 0800 111 0 550.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, Christmas, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal. If sleep problems, deep sadness or a loss of meaning stay with you months later, it is not too late to ask your doctor for help finding a therapist.
  • Other parents who have lived the same can be a great comfort. Support groups are listed below.

You may also find comfort in our guidance for the days after loss.
