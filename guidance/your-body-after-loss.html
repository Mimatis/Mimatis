Your body after loss

You have just given birth. Your body does not know what your heart knows. This guide is for mothers after a late pregnancy loss or neonatal death — covering what to expect physically, and how to care for yourself when caring for yourself feels impossible.

If you experienced an early miscarriage, some of this may still be relevant — but please speak with your GP or midwife about what applies to your situation.

01

Your breasts and milk

Your body will produce milk in the days following birth, driven by the natural hormonal shift after delivery. This happens regardless of whether your baby survived — and it can feel profoundly cruel. You are not alone in finding this one of the most physically and emotionally difficult parts of the days after loss.

Your breasts may become very full, tight, and tender. This is called engorgement and it is temporary, though it can last several days. There are things that can help: applying a cool cloth or cold compress for relief, expressing just enough milk by hand to ease the pressure — not enough to stimulate further production — and wearing a supportive, well-fitting bra.

Warm showers can help milk release naturally and ease discomfort. Speak to your midwife or GP about medication that can help suppress milk production if you would like this option — it is available and completely valid to ask for it.

If you were expressing milk for your baby in a neonatal unit and you wish to continue, some hospitals offer the option of donating to a milk bank. This is a personal choice. There is no right answer.

Talk to your midwife about all available options. You do not need to manage this alone.

02

Bleeding and physical recovery after birth

After birth — whether vaginal or by caesarean section — your body will shed the lining of the womb over the following weeks. This bleeding, known as lochia, is normal and expected. It will typically be heaviest in the first few days, gradually lightening and changing colour from red to pink to brown over two to six weeks.

Use maternity pads rather than tampons during this time, as your body needs to heal internally. If you are passing large clots, bleeding very heavily, or are concerned about anything you are experiencing, contact your midwife or GP. Trust your instincts — you know your body.

Abdominal cramping in the first days after birth is also normal. Your womb is contracting back to its usual size and this can feel like strong period pains. Pain relief such as ibuprofen or paracetamol can help — always follow the recommended dosage and check with your healthcare provider if you are unsure.

03

Healing after a vaginal birth or stitches

If you experienced a tear or episiotomy during birth, you may have stitches that need time to heal. Even though caring for yourself physically may feel unimportant right now, keeping the area clean and dry will help prevent infection and support healing.

Bathing gently in clean, warm water each day is helpful. Lying on your side rather than sitting for extended periods can reduce discomfort in the first days. Try to avoid anything that puts pressure on the area while you heal.

If you have any concerns about your stitches — including unusual pain, swelling, or any signs of infection such as increased redness or discharge — contact your midwife or GP. You are still in your postnatal recovery period and you are entitled to care.

04

Recovering from a caesarean section

If your baby was born by caesarean section, your recovery will take longer and you will need more support at home in the weeks that follow. You may have been in hospital for several days, and returning home without your baby is a pain that no medical guidance can fully address — but your body still needs time and care.

Pain relief will be important in the first days and weeks. Move gently and as soon as you feel able — walking, even slowly, helps the body recover. Avoid lifting anything heavier than your baby would have been for the first six weeks. Driving is usually not advised for around six weeks — check with your doctor.

Keep your wound clean and dry. Gently washing and drying the area each day helps prevent infection. If you notice redness, swelling, discharge, or increased pain around the wound site, contact your GP promptly.

Your midwife team will continue to visit you at home after discharge. Do not hesitate to ask them about anything, physical or emotional.

05

Your pelvic floor

Giving birth, regardless of the outcome, weakens the pelvic floor muscles. You may notice less control than usual, or you may feel very little sensation at all when you first try to engage these muscles. This is completely normal and will improve over time.

Gentle pelvic floor exercises — squeezing and holding the internal muscles as if stopping the flow of urine — can begin whenever you feel ready. There is no pressure or timeline. If bladder control has not improved after three months, speak to your GP about a referral to a physiotherapist who specialises in postnatal recovery.

Be patient with your body. It has been through something enormous. Recovery takes time — physically and in every other way.

06

Sleep, appetite, and the physical effects of grief

Grief is not only emotional. It lives in the body too. In the weeks and months after loss, many parents experience disrupted sleep, changes in appetite, physical exhaustion, headaches, and a lowered immune system. These are normal responses to profound stress and loss — not signs that something is wrong with you.

Try to eat something, even when appetite has gone. Not because food will fix anything, but because your body is healing from birth while also carrying grief, and it needs fuel. Simple, easy foods — whatever you can manage — are better than nothing.

Sleep when you can. Accept help when it is offered. Reduce demands on yourself wherever possible. The months after a loss are not the time to function at full capacity, and no one around you should expect that of you.

If you are finding that physical symptoms of grief are significantly affecting your daily life after several months — persistent insomnia, inability to eat, or physical symptoms you cannot explain — speak to your GP. They can help, and you deserve support.

Up to 60% of bereaved parents show symptoms of depression, anxiety or post-traumatic stress. If you are struggling, please ask for help. You are not being dramatic. You are not weak. You are human.

07

Your first period after loss

Your first period after a birth typically returns within four to eight weeks, though this varies. It can sometimes be difficult to distinguish from the ongoing lochia in the first weeks.

Many parents find their first period after loss unexpectedly hard — emotionally as well as physically. It can feel like another loss, another reminder that time is moving forward when part of you feels it should have stopped. This is a completely understandable response.

Your cycle may not return to its usual pattern straight away. If you have concerns about your cycle or are planning another pregnancy, your GP or a specialist can advise you on what to expect and when it is safe to consider trying again.

08

When to seek medical help

You are still a postnatal patient. You have the right to full postnatal care from your midwife team and GP regardless of what happened to your baby. Do not minimise your physical symptoms or assume you are not entitled to support.

Contact your midwife or GP promptly if you experience: very heavy bleeding or large clots, signs of infection in a wound or the vaginal area, severe or worsening pain, a high temperature, difficulty breathing, or any symptom that feels wrong to you.

Your instincts matter. If something does not feel right, get it checked. You have been through one of the most physically and emotionally demanding experiences a person can face — your body deserves the same care it would receive after any other birth.

Always remember

If you are ever unsure whether something you are experiencing is normal, contact your midwife or GP. You are still a patient and you are entitled to full postnatal care, regardless of whether your baby survived.
