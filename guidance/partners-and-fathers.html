Guidance & support

You are grieving too

Most bereavement resources are written for mothers. This page is written for you — the partner, the father, the person who is also grieving, who may be holding someone else together, and who may have nowhere to put your own pain.

01

Your grief is real — and it may look different

Partners and fathers grieve as deeply as mothers after pregnancy or infant loss. But grief rarely looks the same between two people, and the way men and partners often grieve — quietly, practically, without talking — can make it feel invisible, even to themselves. Your grief is not less because it is quieter. It is not less because you went back to work. It is not less because you are holding someone else together. It is real, and it deserves space.

02

You may feel you have to be strong

The pressure to be the steady one — to manage the practical things, to keep going, to hold your partner — is something almost every father and partner describes. There is nothing wrong with being strong for someone you love. But strength cannot mean silence forever. Find at least one place where you do not have to hold it — whether that is with a trusted friend, a counsellor, or on your own in the car on the way home from work. You need somewhere to put it.

03

Grief can look like anger, distraction, or numbness

Not all grief looks like crying. For many men and partners, grief surfaces as irritability, an inability to concentrate, throwing themselves into work, losing interest in things they used to care about, or a persistent feeling of numbness. These are all forms of grief. If you recognise yourself in any of these and it has been going on for weeks or months, it may be worth speaking to a GP or counsellor who understands bereavement.

04

You may grieve at a different pace than your partner

One of the most common — and most painful — experiences for couples after a loss is discovering that they grieve differently and at different speeds. One of you may want to talk about it constantly; the other may need silence. One may seem to be moving forward while the other is still in the acute phase of grief. Neither of you is wrong. This difference is not a sign that you loved your baby less. But it can create distance between you if it is not acknowledged. Tell each other, as honestly as you can, what you need — and ask what your partner needs in return.

05

You are also a bereaved parent

Much of the medical and support system — the midwife visits, the counselling referrals, the bereavement leaflets — is directed at mothers. This can leave fathers and partners feeling like a secondary figure in a loss that belongs equally to them. You are not secondary. You lost your child too. You are entitled to bereavement leave, to grief counselling, to support. If your employer or GP does not offer it readily, ask for it directly.

06

Returning to work

Many partners return to work within days of a loss — sometimes because there is no formal bereavement leave provision, sometimes because work feels safer than being at home. If you are at work and struggling, you do not have to explain the details to everyone. A simple statement — "I have had a bereavement and I am not fully myself right now" — is enough. You are entitled to compassion from your workplace, and most people will offer it if given the chance.

07

Letter templates are available for you too

The letter templates in the Finding the Right Words section of this site were written with all bereaved parents in mind — including partners and fathers. You may find them helpful when notifying your employer, your wider circle, or when you simply cannot find the words yourself.

08

Where to find support

MIMATIS lists verified support resources across 29 countries in the Find Help section — many of which offer support specifically for fathers and partners. If you find it difficult to seek help for yourself, consider framing it as something you are doing for your relationship or your family. The strongest thing a bereaved parent can do is ask for support.
