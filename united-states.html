We are so sorry that you are here. Whether your baby died early in pregnancy, later in pregnancy, at birth or soon after, your baby matters and your grief is real. In the United States, a loss after 20 weeks is called a stillbirth, and a loss before 20 weeks is usually called a miscarriage. Many families also say “pregnancy loss” or “infant loss”.

This page gathers what parents in the United States need to know, in the order you are likely to need it. Please take only what you need today. The rest will still be here.

MIMATIS shares information and trusted links. It is not legal or medical advice. In the United States, most of the rules are made by each of the 50 states, and some by the federal government. They differ a lot. We say clearly what applies everywhere and what depends on your state, and we tell you what to ask. Please confirm anything important with your hospital team, your state’s vital records office, your employer or your health insurer. Information checked in October 2026.

If you are in danger, or thinking about ending your life, please call or text 988 now, or call 911 in an emergency. The 988 Lifeline is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day. You can also call or text the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline on 1-833-852-6262, any time, in English or Spanish.

Types of pregnancy and baby loss, in simple words

Doctors, hospitals and laws use many different words. This is what they mean in the United States.

Early miscarriage

When a pregnancy ends on its own in the first 13 weeks. It is the most common loss, and it is almost never caused by anything the parents did.

Late miscarriage

When a baby dies in the womb between about 14 and 20 weeks. Doctors may call it a second-trimester loss. The mother often gives birth to her baby.

Stillbirth

When a baby dies in the womb or during birth at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later. This is the definition the CDC uses. Most states then record a fetal death.

Neonatal death

When a baby is born alive and dies in the first 28 days of life, even after only a few minutes.

Sudden infant death (SIDS / SUID)

The sudden, unexpected death of a baby under one year old, usually during sleep. SIDS is when no cause is found. It is no one’s fault.

Ectopic pregnancy

When the pregnancy grows outside the womb, most often in a fallopian tube. It cannot continue, and the mother needs urgent care.

Molar pregnancy

A rare problem with the placenta, so the pregnancy cannot develop. The mother needs check-ups for some months afterwards.

Chemical pregnancy

A very early loss, soon after a positive pregnancy test, often around the time of a period. It is still a loss.

Termination for medical reasons (TFMR)

When parents decide to end a wanted pregnancy because of a serious health problem of the baby or the mother. It is a loss too, and you deserve the same support.

Whatever the word, your baby was real and your grief matters.

The most important thing to know: 20 weeks, and your state

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines a stillbirth as the loss of a pregnancy at or after the 20th week. A miscarriage is usually a loss before 20 weeks. Doctors also say fetal death for both.

For records, most states count a fetal death at 20 weeks or more, or at 350 grams or more. A few states record a fetal death at any stage of pregnancy. Which rules apply to you depends on your state, so there is no single US rule on the paperwork.

What happens in your situation

Early miscarriage

Losing a baby in the first months of pregnancy is very common, and it is still a real loss. Many parents feel alone with it, because others may not have known about the pregnancy. Your grief deserves care and time.

  • Your doctor or midwife will talk with you about your options. Please ask what each one involves and take the time you need.
  • You can ask for an ultrasound print as a last image.
  • If you need time to recover, your doctor can write a note for your employer. You do not have to give details. See time off work.
  • Many families mark an early loss in their own way: lighting a candle, planting a tree, writing a letter, keeping the ultrasound image.
  • You can plant a flower for your baby in our memorial garden.

Stillbirth, 20 weeks or later

When a baby dies in the second or third trimester, the mother still gives birth. This can be a shock. Please know that you can see and hold your baby, and create memories.

  • The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) says that a dilation and evacuation (D&E) can be considered in the second trimester. Labor induction may also be an option, later in the second trimester and in the third. The choice depends on your health and how far along you are. You and your doctor decide together, so ask what you can expect and what your options are.
  • You can ask for tests to look for a cause. ACOG names an examination of the placenta, an autopsy and genetic testing as the most useful. You choose what is done. See at the hospital.
  • A fetal death record is filed with your state, and you may be able to get a certificate. See certificates.
  • You may be able to take unpaid, job-protected leave. See time off work.
  • Your body gives birth as after any birth, including milk coming in. See when you go home.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby lived, even for a short time, your baby is generally recorded as a birth and as a death, as for any person. The exact rules are set by your state, so ask the hospital team for the birth certificate and death certificate. You can hold your baby, bathe and dress your baby and make memories for as long as the team can allow. Many hospitals have a bereavement nurse, a chaplain or a social worker for families in this situation. Please ask.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Unless there is a medical emergency, you do not need to rush. If you learn that your baby has died before birth, you can talk with the team about the timing and about pain relief. Some parents go home for a short time first, to gather the people they need.

These are things you can ask for:

  • To see and hold your baby, for as long as you need. You decide when to hand your baby to the nurse.
  • For family to meet your baby, including brothers and sisters. Children often want to see the baby.
  • A blessing or a baptism, through the hospital chaplaincy, if this matters to you.
  • Someone to describe your baby to you, if you are not ready to look yourself.
  • To understand why. Ask which tests are possible and what each involves, such as the placenta, an autopsy and genetic tests. You can say yes or no to each one. Ask when and how you will receive the results.
  • A bereavement nurse or social worker, if the hospital has one.

You do not have to do any of this. Many parents later say that time with their baby helped them in their grief. Whatever you choose is right for you.

Creating memories

You can ask the team for:

  • Photos — on your own phone, or taken by the staff
  • Hand and foot prints
  • A lock of hair
  • The blanket or clothes your baby wore
  • A memory box, if the hospital has them

Free professional photos: Now I Lay Me Down To Sleep offers free professional portraits to families who lose a baby. You can also ask for portraits if you lost your baby in the past. Find a photographer on the website at nowilaymedowntosleep.org/find-photographers, or call 720-283-3339.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Hospital practice varies across the country. If you feel unheard, you can ask to speak with the charge nurse, the hospital chaplain, a social worker or the patient advocate (many hospitals have a patient relations office). A support organisation can also help you say what you want.

Certificates, records and your baby’s name

In the United States, the death of a baby after about 20 weeks is recorded by the state as a fetal death. Each state has its own rules for the record and for any certificate you can keep.

The fetal death record
  • Most states record a fetal death at 20 weeks or more, or 350 grams or more. A few states record a fetal death at any stage of pregnancy.
  • Ask the hospital team who files the record and how you can get a copy. Ask whether your baby’s name can be on it.
A certificate for you to keep: “Certificate of Birth Resulting in Stillbirth”

Some states let parents request a certificate that says a birth took place and that the baby was stillborn. It does not change legal rights. It is a record of your baby. The Star Legacy Foundation lists 15 states that issue it:

Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana and New Jersey.

  • Several more states issue a certificate of stillbirth: California, Colorado, the District of Columbia, Michigan, Nebraska, Nevada and New Hampshire. Washington State began issuing the certificate on 1 October 2022, with no limit on how long ago the loss happened. More states have changed their rules since, so please check your state’s vital records office.
  • Often only a parent named on the fetal death record can ask. The fee is often small. In the list it ranges from free to about $30. Some states ask for a notarised form.
  • A few states let you leave your baby’s first name blank, or add it. Ask.
  • The certificate is not proof of a live birth.

The Star Legacy Foundation keeps a state-by-state page with how to apply, fees and rules.

If your baby was born alive

Ask the hospital for your baby’s birth certificate and death certificate. You will need the death certificate for a funeral, and sometimes for insurance.

Saying goodbye: funerals

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way, at any stage of pregnancy. In the United States, the rules for what happens to a baby’s body after a loss, especially before 20 weeks, differ by state and by hospital.

  • Ask early. Tell the hospital team as early as you can what you would like: to take your baby home, to bury or cremate your baby, or to let the hospital arrange it.
  • Some hospitals arrange a shared burial or cremation if you do not. Practice differs from hospital to hospital and from state to state, so please ask what yours does.
  • Ask what the hospital charges, if anything.
Your options
  • A burial or cremation arranged by the hospital. Ask whether there is a memorial service or a place you can visit.
  • A funeral home, for a burial or cremation of your own. Costs vary a lot.
  • Your own ceremony: music, readings, a gathering at home or outdoors. A religious service is possible too.
Your rights as a funeral customer

The US Federal Trade Commission’s Funeral Rule gives you these rights with any funeral home:

  • You can ask for a written, itemised price list and keep it to compare.
  • The funeral home must give you prices on the phone if you ask, without asking for your name first.
  • No state law requires routine embalming for every death. Refrigeration is often fine.
  • You can buy a casket or urn elsewhere, and the funeral home cannot refuse it or charge a fee for it.
Places of memory

Many cemeteries, hospitals and parishes hold a memorial service for babies lost in pregnancy, often in October, which is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Awareness Month. October 15 is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Remembrance Day, and every year on the second Sunday of December at 7:00 pm, families around the world light a candle for their children (Worldwide Candle Lighting). Your hospital’s chaplain and local groups can tell you what exists near you.

Time off work and money

You do not have to go back to work while you are in shock. The United States has no national paid leave for pregnancy loss. What you can get depends on your employer, the size of the company and your state. Here is what the law offers.

Federal rules

  • Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA). If you are eligible, you can take up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a year. A Better Balance, a legal advocacy group, explains that pregnancy loss counts as a “serious health condition” under the FMLA. You are eligible if your employer has 50 or more employees within 75 miles, you have worked there for at least 12 months, and you worked at least 1,250 hours in the last 12 months. A partner can also use FMLA leave to care for the mother during her recovery.
  • Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA). Since June 2023, employers with 15 or more employees must give reasonable accommodations for pregnancy, childbirth and related conditions. The EEOC lists miscarriage among them. Leave to recover can be an accommodation, unless it would cause the employer “undue hardship”.
  • Pregnancy Discrimination Act. Employers with 15 or more employees cannot punish you for a pregnancy loss, for example by firing you or cutting your hours.

State rules: paid leave

Some states have paid family and medical leave or disability insurance that pays part of your wages while you are off. As of 2026, these are: California, New Jersey, New York and Rhode Island (with disability insurance), Hawaii (disability insurance), and Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Maine, Maryland (contributions start in 2027), Massachusetts, Minnesota, Oregon, Washington and the District of Columbia. New Hampshire and Vermont have voluntary programs. Many states and cities also require paid sick leave, which can cover time off after a loss. Please check what your state offers and whether pregnancy loss is covered.

State rules: bereavement leave

There is no federal law on bereavement leave. A few states have one. Illinois covers miscarriage and stillbirth for employers with 50 or more employees, up to 10 workdays. California gives up to 5 days of reproductive loss leave after a miscarriage or stillbirth, for employers with 5 or more employees. Other states, such as Oregon, Maryland, Vermont and Washington, have bereavement rules too, and details differ. Laws in this area are changing, so please check your state and ask your employer or the state labor department.

If you need advice

  • A Better Balance gives free legal advice on work and family rights: 1-833-633-3222 (1-833-NEED-ABB).
  • You do not have to tell your employer the details. A doctor’s note is usually enough.
  • For partners: there is no special national leave for partners. You may be able to use FMLA leave, paid sick leave, vacation or an employer policy. Ask your employer.

Taxes

The IRS says you cannot claim a stillborn child as a dependent. A child must be treated as born alive under state or local law, with proof such as a birth certificate. Some states have their own tax benefits after a stillbirth. The Star Legacy Foundation lists examples: Arizona, Arkansas and Minnesota. They often require a Certificate of Birth Resulting in Stillbirth. Rules change, so please ask a tax professional. Star Legacy’s tax information page is a good place to start.

Health insurance and Medicaid

Federal law requires Medicaid to cover pregnancy-related care for at least 60 days after a pregnancy ends. Since 2022, states can extend this to 12 months, and many have. We could not confirm from official sources whether and how this applies after a pregnancy loss in each state, so please ask your state Medicaid office or the hospital’s financial counsellor. If you have bills you do not understand, you can ask the hospital for an itemised bill and for its financial help policy.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in. Ask your doctor or midwife about your options before you leave the hospital. The Star Legacy Foundation has a page on lactation after a loss.
  • A check-up with your ob-gyn or midwife after a few weeks is a good idea. ACOG says to contact your ob-gyn with any questions, and to ask for help finding local support.
  • Mental health support: the PSI HelpLine (Postpartum Support International) is on 1-800-944-4773. You can call or text, in English or Spanish. Leave a message and a trained volunteer calls back. PSI also runs more than 50 free online support groups. The National Maternal Mental Health Hotline is on 1-833-852-6262, 24 hours a day, by phone, text or chat, in English or Spanish. It is for people who are pregnant or just had a baby. Ask them if they can help after a loss.
  • Peer support: the Star Legacy Foundation, SHARE and The Compassionate Friends offer groups, online and in person. See below.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Talking together about how each of you feels can help.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words and including them in remembering usually help.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, the holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal. If sleep problems, deep sadness or a loss of meaning stay with you months later, it is not too late to ask your doctor for help finding a therapist.
  • If you want to try again, ACOG says that if no cause was found and you have no medical condition, the chance of another stillbirth is very low. It suggests talking with your ob-gyn before conceiving, and giving yourself time to grieve.

You may also find comfort in our guidance for the days after loss.
