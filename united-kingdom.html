After the loss of a baby in the UK

Checked information for parents in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland after a miscarriage, a stillbirth or the death of a baby soon after birth: time with your baby, registering your baby, the funeral, leave from work and where to find support.

Not legal or medical advice. Some rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland; we say where. Please confirm anything important with your hospital, your register office or your employer.

In an emergency, call 999. For urgent medical advice, call 111. If you are struggling to cope, call Samaritans free on 116 123, day or night.

Four things that shape your rights

24 weeks

From 24 weeks of pregnancy, a baby born without signs of life is stillborn. The birth is registered, and full maternity leave and pay apply.

52 weeks

Maternity leave after a stillbirth from 24 weeks, with Statutory Maternity Pay for up to 39 weeks if you qualify.

2 weeks

Parental Bereavement Leave for each parent after a stillbirth from 24 weeks or the death of a child.

Any week

In England, you can ask for a free baby loss certificate for a loss before 24 weeks, at any stage and at any time.

The 24-week line is about paperwork. It says nothing about your love or the size of your loss.

Your situation, step by step

Across the UK, the line is 24 weeks of pregnancy. From then on, a baby born without signs of life is stillborn: the birth is registered, and the mother has full maternity leave and pay. A baby born alive at any stage, who then dies, is registered as a birth and a death.

Registration deadlines: 42 days in England and Wales, 21 days in Scotland, and up to 1 year in Northern Ireland.

Miscarriage, before 24 weeks

Losing a baby in the first months of pregnancy is common, and it is still a real loss. Many parents feel alone with it, because others may not have known about the pregnancy.

  • Your midwife, GP or early pregnancy unit will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • If you need time off, your GP can give you a fit note. Sickness related to pregnancy must not be held against you at work.
  • A baby loss certificate: if you live in England, you can ask for a free certificate for a loss before 24 weeks, with your baby’s name if you wish. It is not a legal document, but many parents value it. See documents and your baby’s name.
  • Saying goodbye: the hospital should tell you the options for your baby’s remains: a shared hospital cremation or burial, your own funeral, or taking your baby home. You do not have to decide straight away. See saying goodbye.
  • From April 2027, both parents will have a right to up to 2 weeks of unpaid bereavement leave after a pregnancy loss before 24 weeks.
  • Seek care quickly if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 999 in an emergency.

Stillbirth, from 24 weeks

When a baby dies before birth from 24 weeks, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. You can see and hold your baby and create memories, as after any birth. See at the hospital.

  • Registering the stillbirth at the register office, with your baby’s name if you wish: within 42 days in England and Wales, 21 days in Scotland, and up to 1 year in Northern Ireland.
  • Mother: 52 weeks of maternity leave. Statutory Maternity Pay for up to 39 weeks if you have worked long enough, or Maternity Allowance if not. Claim within 28 days.
  • Partner: paternity leave, and 2 weeks of Parental Bereavement Leave, which can be taken within 56 weeks.
  • Funeral costs: help is available in every part of the UK. See saying goodbye.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby showed any sign of life, even for a very short time, the birth and the death are both registered. Maternity leave and pay, paternity leave and Parental Bereavement Leave apply, and the same help with funeral costs is available.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Unless there is a medical emergency, you do not need to rush. Most hospitals have a bereavement midwife or team. You can ask for:

  • Time with your baby, as long as you need. Many hospitals have a cold cot so your baby can stay with you.
  • A private room away from rooms with newborn babies.
  • Memories: photos, hand and foot prints, a lock of hair and a memory box. Remember My Baby offers free professional remembrance photography across the UK.
  • To name your baby, dress them, bathe them, and have family, including brothers and sisters, meet them.
  • To understand why: tests such as a post-mortem and examination of the placenta. Ask what is suggested and when you will hear the results. The decision is yours.
  • A chaplain or faith leader, for any faith or none.

You are not obliged to do any of this. Many parents later say that time with their baby helped them in their grief. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Ask to speak with the bereavement midwife or the senior midwife on duty. Every NHS hospital has a patient advice service (PALS in England) you can contact.

Saying goodbye: funerals and help with costs

Take the time you need to think about what you want. You can arrange a funeral yourself or through a funeral director, with or without a religious ceremony.

  • England: the Children’s Funeral Fund pays burial or cremation fees and up to £300 for a coffin, for a stillbirth from 24 weeks or a child under 18. It is not means-tested. The provider claims it, so you should not be charged.
  • Wales: councils waive their fees for children’s burials and cremations (ask whether this covers your baby), and families who register a stillbirth from 24 weeks or a child’s death can receive £500 towards costs. It is not means-tested.
  • Scotland: Funeral Support Payment can help if you get certain benefits. Ask your council about its fees for a baby’s funeral.
  • Northern Ireland: a Child Funeral Fund helps families after a stillbirth from 24 weeks or the death of a child.
  • Before 24 weeks: hospitals offer a shared cremation or burial, or you can arrange your own farewell or take your baby home. Ask the hospital to explain your options.

Documents and your baby’s name

  • From 24 weeks, the stillbirth is registered at the register office, and you can give your baby a name. You receive a certificate of stillbirth.
  • Before 24 weeks, in England: a free baby loss certificate for each baby, with a name or nickname, sex, date and place if you wish. Ask online or by phone, 0300 330 9445. It can include the other parent’s name, with their permission.
  • The baby loss certificate is for families in England. In Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, ask your hospital or a charity: many offer a commemorative certificate.
  • If your baby was born alive, both a birth and a death certificate are made.

If something is unclear, ask your local register office.

Time off work

You do not have to go back to work while you are in shock. This is what UK law provides.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. Your community midwife and GP will see you. Seek care if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • A follow-up appointment with the consultant goes through what happened and any test results. Ask when it will be.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, Christmas, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • Baby Loss Awareness Week ends on 15 October with the Wave of Light: many families light a candle at 7 pm.
