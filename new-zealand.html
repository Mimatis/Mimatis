We are so sorry that you are here. Whether your baby died early in pregnancy, later in pregnancy, at birth or soon after, your baby matters and your grief is real.

This page gathers what parents in New Zealand need to know, in the order you are likely to need it. Please take only what you need today. The rest will still be here.

Types of pregnancy and baby loss, in simple words

Doctors, hospitals and laws use many different words. This is what they mean in New Zealand.

Early miscarriage

When a pregnancy ends on its own in the first 13 weeks. It is the most common loss, and it is almost never caused by anything the parents did.

Late miscarriage

When a baby dies in the womb between about 14 and 20 weeks. Doctors may call it a second-trimester loss. The mother often gives birth to her baby.

Stillbirth

In New Zealand, when a baby is born without signs of life after 20 weeks of pregnancy, or weighing 400 grams or more if the dates are uncertain.

Neonatal death

When a baby is born alive and dies in the first 28 days of life, even after only a few minutes.

Sudden infant death (SIDS / SUID)

The sudden, unexpected death of a baby under one year old, usually during sleep. SIDS is when no cause is found. It is no one’s fault.

Ectopic pregnancy

When the pregnancy grows outside the womb, most often in a fallopian tube. It cannot continue, and the mother needs urgent care.

Molar pregnancy

A rare problem with the placenta, so the pregnancy cannot develop. The mother needs check-ups for some months afterwards.

Chemical pregnancy

A very early loss, soon after a positive pregnancy test, often around the time of a period. It is still a loss.

Termination for medical reasons (TFMR)

When parents decide to end a wanted pregnancy because of a serious health problem of the baby or the mother. It is a loss too, and you deserve the same support.

Whatever the word, your baby was real and your grief matters.

Common questions

Is it a miscarriage or a stillbirth in New Zealand?

A baby born without signs of life after 20 weeks of pregnancy, or weighing 400 grams or more if the dates are uncertain, is stillborn. An earlier loss is a miscarriage. Read more →

Does a stillborn baby have to be registered?

Yes. After 20 weeks, you must register your baby’s birth, within 2 months. Registration is free, and you can buy a birth certificate afterwards. Read more →

Is there leave from work after a miscarriage or stillbirth?

Yes. Employees who have worked for their employer for six months can take 3 days of paid bereavement leave after a miscarriage or stillbirth. Partners can take it too. Read more →

Do I keep parental leave if my baby dies?

If you are eligible for primary carer leave and parental leave payments, you stay eligible if you have a miscarriage or your baby is stillborn or dies. Primary carer leave is 26 weeks. Read more →

Where can I find support in New Zealand?

Sands New Zealand is a network of parent-run groups for families after the death of a baby. You can free call or text 1737 at any time to talk with a trained counsellor. Read more →

The most important thing to know: 20 weeks or 400 grams

In New Zealand, a baby born without signs of life after 20 weeks of pregnancy, or weighing 400 grams or more if the dates are uncertain, is stillborn. A loss before that is a miscarriage.

What happens in your situation

Miscarriage

Losing a baby in pregnancy is common, and it is still a real loss.

  • Your midwife or doctor will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • There is no legal requirement to register your baby’s birth or to bury your baby. You are still welcome to hold a funeral.
  • Leave: 3 days of paid bereavement leave, and parental leave if you were eligible. See leave and money.
  • Seek care straight away if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 111 in an emergency.

Stillbirth

When a baby dies before birth after 20 weeks, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. Seeing and holding your baby is your choice. See at the hospital.

  • Registration: you must register your baby’s birth within 2 months. See registration.
  • Burial or cremation is required. See saying goodbye.
  • Leave: bereavement leave and parental leave. See leave and money.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby dies in the first 28 days, you must register both the birth and the death, and your baby must be buried or cremated. If you are eligible for paid parental leave, you are still entitled to it. Inland Revenue asks for a letter from your doctor or midwife confirming your baby’s date of birth.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Your health and safety come first. Always follow your medical team.

Health New Zealand tells parents:

  • Seeing and holding your baby is your choice. You will be given privacy and as much time as you want.
  • Memories: photos, foot and hand prints, a cot name card and a name bracelet. Once the birth is registered, you can also have a birth certificate.
  • A ceremony: you can hold one, and a chaplain or your own religious leader may be available.
  • A post-mortem is a gentle examination to better understand why your baby died. Parents usually decide and sign the consent, unless a coroner is involved. Results usually take 8 to 10 weeks, and you can be offered a meeting to talk about them.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Ask to speak with your midwife, the charge midwife or the hospital’s bereavement service. A support organisation such as Sands can also help you find the words.

Registration and your baby’s name

  • Before 20 weeks, there is no legal requirement to register your baby’s birth.
  • After 20 weeks, you must register your baby’s birth with Births, Deaths and Marriages, within 2 months of the birth. A medical certificate is completed at the hospital for you or your funeral director.
  • If your baby was born alive and died in the first 28 days, you must register both the birth and the death.
  • Registering is free. You can buy certificates afterwards.

If you are not sure which rule applies to you, ask your midwife or Births, Deaths and Marriages.

Saying goodbye: burial and cremation

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way.

  • After 20 weeks, and when a baby dies after birth, your baby must be buried or cremated.
  • Before 20 weeks, there is no legal requirement, and you are still welcome to hold a funeral.
  • Help with the cost: Work and Income may be able to give a funeral grant to a parent. It depends on your income and assets. Call 0800 559 009.
  • Sands New Zealand has leaflets for parents, including “Your Baby’s Funeral” and “Transporting Your Baby”.

Tell your midwife or the hospital team what you want as early as you can.

Leave from work and money

Bereavement leave

  • 3 days of paid bereavement leave after a miscarriage or stillbirth.
  • Partners can take it too, and so can people who were planning to have a child through adoption or surrogacy.
  • Who is eligible: employees, including part-time and casual employees, who have worked for the employer for six months. Check the conditions with Employment New Zealand.

Parental leave and payments

  • If you are eligible for primary carer leave and have a miscarriage, or your baby is stillborn or dies, you are still entitled to 26 weeks of leave.
  • If you are eligible for parental leave payments, you stay eligible even if your baby dies before or after birth. Payments come from Inland Revenue.
  • You can apply afterwards: if the loss happens before you applied for primary carer leave, you can still apply.
  • Going back early: you can return to work earlier than planned without needing your employer’s approval.

Ask your employer what your employment agreement adds. Some employers offer more than the legal minimum.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. Seek care straight away if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. Children and young people aged 5 to 19 can call What’s Up on 0800 942 8787. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • 15 October is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Remembrance Day. Many families light a candle at 7 pm.

You may also find comfort in our guidance for the days after loss.

You can plant a flower for your baby in our memorial garden. It stays there, with your baby’s name.

Health New Zealand advises calling your midwife or doctor if bleeding stays heavy or has clots, or if you have a fever, tummy pain or a smelly discharge, and having a check-up about 6 weeks after the birth.
