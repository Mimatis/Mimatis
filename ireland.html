After the loss of a baby in Ireland

Checked information for parents in Ireland after a miscarriage, a stillbirth or the death of a baby soon after birth: time with your baby, registering your baby, the funeral, leave from work and where to find support.

Not legal or medical advice. Please confirm anything important with your hospital’s bereavement team, the civil registration office or your employer.

In an emergency, call 112 or 999. If you have thoughts of not wanting to live, call Pieta on 1800 247 247 or Samaritans on 116 123, free, day and night.

Four things that shape your rights

23 weeks

Since September 2024, a baby born without signs of life from 23 weeks or 400 g is stillborn, and can be registered.

26 weeks

After a stillbirth, the mother keeps her full maternity leave and benefit, plus 16 weeks of unpaid leave if she wants it.

2 weeks

The partner keeps the 2 weeks of paternity leave after a stillbirth.

Every unit

Each maternity unit should have a bereavement team, with a specialist midwife.

These lines are about paperwork. They say nothing about your love or the size of your loss.

Your situation, step by step

In Ireland, the line is 23 weeks of pregnancy or 400 g. It was lowered from 24 weeks and 500 g on 16 September 2024. A baby born without signs of life past that line is stillborn. A loss before it is a miscarriage. If your baby showed any sign of life, at any stage, your baby was born alive.

Miscarriage, before 23 weeks

Losing a baby in pregnancy is common, and it is still a real loss. Many parents feel alone with it, because others may not have known about the pregnancy.

  • Your doctor or midwife will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • If you need time off, your doctor can certify you as unfit for work, and your employer pays statutory sick pay for a limited number of days a year, at 70% of your pay up to €110 a day, after 13 weeks of service.
  • Saying goodbye: ask the bereavement team what the hospital offers, including a hospital burial or arranging your own. Tell them what you want, as early as you can.
  • Talk to others: the Miscarriage Association of Ireland runs a volunteer phone line and monthly meetings in Dublin.
  • Seek care quickly if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 112 or 999 in an emergency.

Stillbirth, from 23 weeks

When a baby dies before birth from 23 weeks, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. You can see and hold your baby and create memories, as after any birth. See at the hospital.

  • Registration is your choice, at any civil registration office, with photo ID and the hospital’s evidence. It is free, the register includes your baby’s name, and you usually receive a certificate the same day.
  • The Record of Stillbirths: since September 2024 you can also ask to add your baby to a public record, if you wish.
  • A funeral: the hospital must explain your options and their costs, both hospital and private. See saying goodbye.
  • Mother: full maternity leave of 26 weeks, with Maternity Benefit if you have enough PRSI contributions, plus 16 weeks of unpaid leave.
  • Partner: 2 weeks of paternity leave, with Paternity Benefit if you qualify.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby showed any sign of life, even for a very short time, the birth and the death are both registered. The same maternity and paternity leave apply. The bereavement team will explain the funeral options.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Unless there is a medical emergency, you do not need to rush. The HSE National Standards say you should be offered:

  • Time to see and hold your baby, and another chance later if you were not ready at first.
  • Memories: hand and foot prints, a lock of hair, washing and dressing your baby, a memory box. Many hospitals have memory boxes from Féileacáin.
  • Photographs taken by staff, and a professional photographer where available.
  • Brothers and sisters included in making memories, if you wish.
  • Written information, a named health professional and a phone number to call.
  • To understand why: ask whether tests, such as a post-mortem and an examination of the placenta, are possible, and when you will hear the results.

You are not obliged to do any of this. Many parents later say that time with their baby helped them in their grief. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Ask to speak with the bereavement midwife, the consultant or the chaplain. You can also give feedback or make a complaint through the HSE’s Your Service, Your Say.

Saying goodbye: burial and cremation

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way. Take the time you need to think about what you want.

  • The hospital must give you spoken and written information about funeral options, and explain the costs of a hospital burial and a private one.
  • You can arrange a service, a burial or a cremation, with the hospital’s help or through a funeral director.
  • Small things matter: you can place a note, a toy or a keepsake with your baby.

Documents and your baby’s name

  • Before 23 weeks, there is no civil registration.
  • From 23 weeks or 400 g, you can register your baby’s stillbirth, with first name and surname. It is free and voluntary, and you can do it later too. Stillbirths since 1995 can be registered, and earlier ones with evidence.
  • If your baby was born alive and then died, both the birth and the death are registered.

Questions: contact the General Register Office at gro@welfare.ie.

Time off work

You do not have to go back to work while you are in shock. This is what Irish law provides.

  • There is no statutory bereavement leave in Ireland yet. Ask your employer about compassionate leave.
  • If you are not ready to work after your leave, talk to your doctor.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. Seek care if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • Talk to other parents: Féileacáin and A Little Lifetime Foundation offer counselling, peer support and groups.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. Children can call Childline on 1800 66 66 66. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • October brings Baby Loss Awareness Week, and 15 October is the day of remembrance. Many families light a candle at 7 pm.
