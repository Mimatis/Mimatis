After the loss of a baby in Belgium

Checked information for parents in Belgium after a miscarriage, a stillbirth or the death of a baby soon after birth: time with your baby, registering your baby, the funeral, leave from work and where to find support.

Not legal or medical advice. Please confirm anything important with the hospital, your municipality or your child benefit fund. Dutch and French terms are given in brackets, as you will see them on forms.

In an emergency, call 112. If you have thoughts of not wanting to live, call 1813 (Dutch) or 0800 32 123 (French), free, day and night.

Four things that shape your rights

140 days

From 140 days of pregnancy (about 20 weeks), you may register your baby at your municipality, with a first name. It is your choice.

180 days

From 180 days (about 26 weeks), registration is required, and burial or cremation follows. Full leave applies.

10 days

Bereavement leave for each parent after a stillbirth from 180 days or the death of a child, on top of maternity and partner leave.

Any week

You may bury or cremate your baby at any stage. Many municipalities have a star garden for babies.

These lines are about paperwork. They say nothing about your love or the size of your loss.

Your situation, step by step

Belgium has two lines in pregnancy. From 140 days (about 20 weeks), you may register your stillborn baby with a first name. From 180 days (about 26 weeks), registration is required, burial or cremation must follow, and the parents have full leave. Before 140 days, there is no legal registration, but you still have the right to a burial.

Miscarriage or earlier loss, before 180 days

Losing a baby before 180 days is common, and it is still a real loss. Many parents feel alone with it, because others may not have known about the pregnancy.

  • Your gynaecologist or midwife will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • If you need time off, your doctor can give you sick leave. Flemish government and local authority staff have 2 days of leave after a pregnancy loss before 180 days.
  • From 140 days, you may register your baby at the municipality where your baby was born, with a first name, based on a medical certificate. It is your choice.
  • Saying goodbye: you have the right to bury or cremate your baby at any stage, but it is not required. Hospital practice differs; some hospitals arrange an anonymous burial. Tell the hospital what you want, as early as you can.
  • Seek care quickly if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 112 in an emergency.

Stillbirth, from 180 days

When a baby dies before birth from 180 days, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. You can see and hold your baby and create memories, as after any birth. See at the hospital.

  • Registration at the municipality where your baby was born is required (akte van een levenloos kind / acte d’enfant sans vie). You can give a first name and a family name.
  • Burial or cremation is required, through a funeral director. See saying goodbye.
  • Mother: 15 weeks of maternity leave (17 for twins).
  • Partner: 20 days of birth leave (geboorteverlof / congé de naissance).
  • Both parents: 10 days of bereavement leave (rouwverlof / congé de deuil).
  • Birth allowance: the starting amount (startbedrag) in Flanders, or the birth allowance (prime de naissance) in Brussels and Wallonia. Send the certificate to your child benefit fund.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby showed any sign of life, even for a very short time, the birth and the death are both registered. The same leave applies: 15 weeks for the mother, 20 days for the partner and 10 days of bereavement leave. Burial or cremation is required.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Unless there is a medical emergency, you do not need to rush. You can ask for:

  • Time with your baby, as long as you need, alone or with family.
  • A private room away from mothers with newborn babies.
  • Memories: photos, hand and foot prints, a lock of hair and the ID band. Some hospitals have a Koesterkoffer from Berrefonds.
  • Free professional photographs from Boven De Wolken, across Belgium. Ask the nurses, or request a session yourself.
  • To name your baby, dress them, and have family meet them. You may also take your baby home to say goodbye.
  • To understand why: ask whether tests, such as a post-mortem and an examination of the placenta, are possible, and when you will hear the results.

You are not obliged to do any of this. Many parents later say that time with their baby helped them in their grief. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Ask to speak with the doctor or midwife in charge, or the hospital social worker. Every Belgian hospital has an ombudsperson for patients.

Saying goodbye: burial and cremation

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way. Take the time you need to think about what you want.

  • From 180 days, burial or cremation is required, through a funeral director, in an official cemetery. You can still do much yourself: the death notice, the cards, a viewing at home.
  • Before 180 days, you have the right to a burial or cremation, but it is not required. Ask the hospital what it will do if you do not arrange it yourself.
  • Star gardens: municipalities must have a place for babies in the cemetery: in Flanders from 12 weeks of pregnancy, in Wallonia from 14 weeks and in Brussels from 16 weeks.
  • Ashes may be kept at home, or scattered on private land with the owner’s permission.

Handmade heart-shaped wooden caskets and urns for babies are also made by Hart van Hout.

Documents and your baby’s name

  • Before 140 days, there is no legal registration. Some municipalities keep a symbolic star register.
  • From 140 days, you may register your baby, with a first name. There is no time limit: you can also register a loss from years ago.
  • From 180 days, registration is required, and you can give a first name and a family name. Your baby is recorded in the civil registers.
  • If your baby was born alive and then died, both the birth and the death are registered.

If something is unclear, ask the civil registry of the municipality where your baby was born.

Time off work

You do not have to go back to work while you are in shock. This is what Belgian law provides.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. A midwife can visit you at home. Seek care if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • Talk to other parents: Met Lege Handen and Berrefonds in Flanders, and Mizuko in Brussels, bring bereaved parents together.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. Children can call Awel on 102 or Écoute-Enfants on 103. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • 15 October is the day of remembrance for pregnancy and baby loss. Many families light a candle at 7 pm.
