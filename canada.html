We are so sorry that you are here. Whether your baby died early in pregnancy, later in pregnancy, at birth or soon after, your baby matters and your grief is real.

This page gathers what parents in Canada need to know, in the order you are likely to need it. Please take only what you need today. The rest will still be here.

MIMATIS shares information and trusted links. It is not legal or medical advice. In Canada, most of the rules are made by each province and territory, and some by the federal government. We say clearly what applies across Canada and what depends on where you live, and we tell you what to ask. Please confirm anything important with your hospital team, your province’s vital statistics office, your employer or Service Canada. Information checked in November 2026.

Types of pregnancy and baby loss, in simple words

Doctors, hospitals and laws use many different words. This is what they mean in Canada.

Early miscarriage

When a pregnancy ends on its own in the first 13 weeks. It is the most common loss, and it is almost never caused by anything the parents did.

Late miscarriage

When a baby dies in the womb between about 14 and 20 weeks. Doctors may call it a second-trimester loss. The mother often gives birth to her baby.

Stillbirth

In most of Canada, when a baby is born without signs of life at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later, or weighing 500 grams or more. In Quebec, only the weight of 500 grams counts.

Neonatal death

When a baby is born alive and dies in the first 28 days of life, even after only a few minutes.

Sudden infant death (SIDS / SUID)

The sudden, unexpected death of a baby under one year old, usually during sleep. SIDS is when no cause is found. It is no one’s fault.

Ectopic pregnancy

When the pregnancy grows outside the womb, most often in a fallopian tube. It cannot continue, and the mother needs urgent care.

Molar pregnancy

A rare problem with the placenta, so the pregnancy cannot develop. The mother needs check-ups for some months afterwards.

Chemical pregnancy

A very early loss, soon after a positive pregnancy test, often around the time of a period. It is still a loss.

Termination for medical reasons (TFMR)

When parents decide to end a wanted pregnancy because of a serious health problem of the baby or the mother. It is a loss too, and you deserve the same support.

Whatever the word, your baby was real and your grief matters.

Common questions

Is it a miscarriage or a stillbirth in Canada?

In most of Canada, a baby born without signs of life at 20 weeks or later, or weighing 500 grams or more, is a stillbirth. In Quebec, only the weight of 500 grams counts. An earlier loss is usually called a miscarriage. Read more →

Is there leave from work after a pregnancy loss?

In federally regulated workplaces, there are up to 8 weeks of leave after a stillbirth and 3 days after any other pregnancy loss. Other workplaces follow the rules of their province or territory. Read more →

Can I get Employment Insurance (EI) after a loss?

If your pregnancy ends before week 20, you could receive EI sickness benefits. If it ends in week 20 or later, you could receive EI maternity benefits. Parental benefits are not available. Read more →

Is a stillborn baby registered?

Yes. A stillbirth is registered with the vital statistics office of your province or territory. Ask the hospital and that office what document you can receive, and whether it can show your baby’s name. Read more →

Where can I find support in Canada?

Baby’s Breath offers peer bereavement support across Canada, and the PAIL Network offers peer support in Ontario. If you are thinking about suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Read more →

The most important thing to know: 20 weeks or 500 grams

In most of Canada, a baby born without signs of life at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later, or weighing 500 grams or more, is a stillbirth. In Quebec, only the weight of 500 grams counts. A loss before that line is usually called a miscarriage.

If your baby showed any sign of life after birth, at any stage of pregnancy, your baby was born alive.

What happens in your situation

Miscarriage

Losing a baby in pregnancy is common, and it is still a real loss. Canada’s national maternity care guidelines say that about 15% to 25% of pregnancies end in miscarriage.

  • Your doctor or midwife will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • If you cannot work, you could receive EI sickness benefits. See work and EI.
  • Saying goodbye: ask the hospital what it offers. Tell them what you want as early as you can.
  • Seek care straight away if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 911 in an emergency.

Stillbirth

When a baby dies before birth later in pregnancy, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. You can ask to see and hold your baby and to create memories. See at the hospital.

  • Registration: the stillbirth is registered with your province or territory. See registration.
  • Burial or cremation: the hospital should explain all your options. See saying goodbye.
  • Work: EI maternity benefits if you qualify, and leave that depends on your workplace. See work and EI.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby lived, even for a very short time, you are still entitled to up to 15 weeks of EI maternity benefits if you qualify. Before you leave, the hospital should tell you how the birth and the death are registered, and explain the funeral options.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Your health and safety come first. Always follow your medical team.

Canada’s national guidelines for maternity and newborn care (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2020) say that staff should:

  • Ask whether you want to see or hold your baby, and not limit that time if you do. Some hospitals have a cooling cot, so parents can spend more time with their baby.
  • Offer memories: photos, hand and foot prints, a lock of hair, clothing, a memory box. They should use your baby’s name if you have chosen one. You can say no to any of this.
  • Include brothers and sisters, with clear words for their age, and help grandparents understand how to support you.
  • Offer a post-mortem to all parents and explain the options, including a limited examination. Your consent is needed, except where the law of your province or territory requires one. Ask when you will hear the results.
  • Give you written information about support services, and offer follow-up care after you go home.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Hospital practice differs across the country. You can ask to speak with the nurse in charge, a social worker, spiritual care or the hospital’s patient relations office. A support organisation can also help you find the words.

Registration and your baby’s name

Births, deaths and stillbirths are registered by each province and territory, not by the federal government.

  • A stillbirth is registered with the vital statistics office of your province or territory. A doctor or coroner completes a medical certificate, and parents are usually asked to fill in a registration form.
  • Ask what you can receive. The documents differ between provinces. Ask the hospital and the vital statistics office what document you can order, and whether it can show your baby’s name.
  • Before the 20-week or 500-gram line, a loss is not registered as a stillbirth. Ask the hospital whether it offers a keepsake certificate or another way to record your baby’s name.
  • If your baby was born alive, ask the hospital how the birth and the death are registered. They should tell you before you go home.

Saying goodbye: burial and cremation

You can say goodbye to your baby in your own way. The rules for burial and cremation are different in each province and territory, so the hospital team is the best place to start.

The national guidelines say staff should:

  • Explain all your options, both those arranged by the hospital and private ones.
  • Refer you to funeral homes and tell you about any financial help.
  • Tell you how long your baby can stay in the hospital’s care if you have not decided yet.

Tell the team what you want as early as you can. You can ask to place a note, a toy or a keepsake with your baby.

Time off work and Employment Insurance

Your rights depend on two things: Employment Insurance (EI), which is federal, and the leave rules of your workplace, which are federal for some employers and provincial or territorial for the others.

Employment Insurance (EI)

  • If your pregnancy ends before week 20, you could receive EI sickness benefits if you cannot work for medical reasons. They can last up to 26 weeks.
  • If your pregnancy ends in week 20 or later, you could receive EI maternity benefits, for up to 15 weeks.
  • If your baby dies after birth, you are still entitled to up to 15 weeks of maternity benefits. Tell Service Canada as soon as you can.
  • Parental benefits are not available after a loss.
  • You must meet the conditions for each benefit. Contact Service Canada about your own situation.

If you work in a federally regulated workplace

Since 12 December 2025, the Canada Labour Code gives a leave related to pregnancy loss. Ask your employer whether your workplace is federally regulated.

  • Up to 8 weeks after a stillbirth (from 20 weeks or 500 grams), and 3 days after any other pregnancy loss.
  • The first 3 days are paid if you have worked for the employer for 3 months in a row.
  • Who can take it: the person who was pregnant, their spouse or common-law partner, and intended parents.
  • When: within 26 weeks of the loss, in one or two periods. You give written notice; no other document is required.
  • If your child dies, bereavement leave is up to 8 weeks, with the first 3 days paid after 3 months of work.

If your workplace follows provincial or territorial rules

Each province and territory has its own employment standards, and they differ. Two examples:

  • Ontario: according to Community Legal Education Ontario, pregnancy leave can continue for at least 12 weeks after a miscarriage or stillbirth.
  • Quebec has its own parental insurance plan (QPIP) in place of EI maternity benefits. Ask the CNESST and QPIP what applies to you.

Ask your employer or your province’s employment standards office: what leave applies after a miscarriage or stillbirth, whether any of it is paid, and what applies to your partner.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. Seek care straight away if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. Children and teenagers can call Kids Help Phone on 1-800-668-6868 or text CONNECT to 686868. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • 15 October is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Remembrance Day. Many families light a candle at 7 pm.

You may also find comfort in our guidance for the days after loss.

You can plant a flower for your baby in our memorial garden. It stays there, with your baby’s name.
