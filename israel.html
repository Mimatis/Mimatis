Types of pregnancy and baby loss, in simple words

Doctors, hospitals and laws use many different words. This is what they mean in Israel.

Early miscarriage

When a pregnancy ends on its own in the first 13 weeks. It is the most common loss, and it is almost never caused by anything the parents did.

Late miscarriage

When a baby dies in the womb between about 14 and 22 weeks. Doctors may call it a second-trimester loss. The mother often gives birth to her baby.

Stillbirth

In Israel, when a baby is born without signs of life after 22 full weeks of pregnancy. In Hebrew it is often called leida shketa, a silent birth.

Neonatal death

When a baby is born alive and dies in the first 28 days of life, even after only a few minutes.

Sudden infant death (SIDS / SUID)

The sudden, unexpected death of a baby under one year old, usually during sleep. SIDS is when no cause is found. It is no one’s fault.

Ectopic pregnancy

When the pregnancy grows outside the womb, most often in a fallopian tube. It cannot continue, and the mother needs urgent care.

Molar pregnancy

A rare problem with the placenta, so the pregnancy cannot develop. The mother needs check-ups for some months afterwards.

Chemical pregnancy

A very early loss, soon after a positive pregnancy test, often around the time of a period. It is still a loss.

Termination for medical reasons (TFMR)

When parents decide to end a wanted pregnancy because of a serious health problem of the baby or the mother. It is a loss too, and you deserve the same support.

Whatever the word, your baby was real and your grief matters.

Common questions

Is it a miscarriage or a silent birth (stillbirth) in Israel?

For National Insurance, a baby born without signs of life after 22 full weeks of pregnancy is a stillbirth, often called a silent birth. An earlier loss is usually called a miscarriage. Read more →

Am I entitled to maternity leave after a stillbirth?

Yes. After 22 weeks, a mother has the same maternity leave as after a live birth, and maternity allowance from National Insurance if she meets the conditions. Read more →

Can we be at our baby’s burial?

The most important thing to know: 22 weeks, and 20 weeks

In Israel, a baby born without signs of life after 22 full weeks of pregnancy is a stillbirth, often called a silent birth (leida shketa). From then on, National Insurance treats it like any birth. A loss before that is usually called a miscarriage.

What happens in your situation

Miscarriage

Losing a baby in pregnancy is common, and it is still a real loss.

  • Your doctor or the hospital team will explain what is happening and your options. Ask as many questions as you need.
  • Work: you can be absent for a week, paid from your sick days, and longer if your doctor says so. See leave and money.
  • After 20 weeks, or if your baby weighed more than 500 grams, you can choose how involved you want to be in the farewell and the burial. See saying goodbye.
  • Seek care straight away if the bleeding becomes heavy, if you have strong pain, or if you have a fever. Call 101 in an emergency.

Silent birth (stillbirth)

When a baby dies before birth, the mother usually gives birth to her baby. Seeing and holding your baby is your choice. See at the hospital.

  • After 22 weeks, you have maternity leave and National Insurance benefits as after any birth. See leave and money.
  • Burial: you choose how involved you want to be. See saying goodbye.

If your baby was born alive and then died

If your baby dies in the first 30 days, or later without ever leaving the hospital, you have the same right to take part in the farewell and the burial. National Insurance says a mother whose baby died after birth is entitled to the birth benefits, under the usual conditions.

At the hospital: time with your baby

Your health and safety come first. Always follow your medical team.

  • If your baby has died in the womb, you are entitled to give birth as soon as possible, and at the latest within 3 days of the diagnosis, under a Ministry of Health circular.
  • Seeing and holding your baby is your choice. Hillel Yaffe Medical Center, for example, encourages parents to see and hold their baby and to give a name, and supports whatever parents decide.
  • Memories: many hospitals offer a photo, hand and foot prints and a memory box. Ask the midwife or the social worker.
  • A post-mortem may help to understand why your baby died. It needs the consent of one parent, as long as the other parent does not object.
  • Breast milk: some mothers choose to donate their milk to the national breast milk bank. Ask the midwife if you would like to.
  • A social worker is usually part of the team. You can ask to see one at any time.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

If you feel your wishes are not respected

Ask to speak with the senior midwife, the social worker or the head of the department. A support organisation such as Nitzotzot Inbar can also help you find the words.

Records and your baby’s name

A baby who is born alive is registered in the population register, even if they die soon after. The rules for recording a baby born without signs of life are different.

  • Ask the hospital social worker what is recorded about your baby, which documents you will receive, and whether your baby’s name can be written on them.
  • For National Insurance, the hospital’s confirmation of the birth after 22 weeks is what matters for the birth grant and the maternity allowance.
  • Giving your baby a name is always your choice, whatever the documents say.

Saying goodbye: the farewell and the burial

For many years, babies in Israel were often buried without their parents. Today the rules give parents a choice.

If your baby died after 20 weeks of pregnancy, or weighed more than 500 grams, or died in the first 30 days, a hospital representative will explain three options:

  • The hospital arranges the burial, without the family.
  • You take part in the farewell at the hospital, and the hospital arranges the burial with the burial society.
  • You take part in the whole burial, civil or religious, as you wish. The hospital refers you to a burial society that buries babies.

Your choice is written down on a form. Hillel Yaffe Medical Center explains that in a Jewish burial from 22 weeks, babies are buried in a designated area: you can ask where, visit, and ask to be present. In Muslim families, the family performs the burial. Tell the team about any religious needs.

If you need help finding out where your baby is buried, or with questions about religious burial, the organisation ITIM can help.

Leave from work and money

After a silent birth (after 22 weeks)

  • Maternity leave: the same as after a live birth. You may return to work earlier, from 3 weeks after the birth, if you wish.
  • Maternity allowance (dmei leida) from National Insurance: up to 15 weeks if you paid insurance contributions for 10 of the last 14 months, or 15 of the last 22. With 6 of the last 14 months, it is paid for 8 weeks.
  • Birth grant and hospitalization grant from National Insurance.
  • Your partner may also take maternity leave after a silent birth, but no maternity allowance is paid for that leave.
  • Protection from dismissal applies to you, and also to your partner, as after any birth.

If you want to go back to work during the maternity allowance period, contact your National Insurance branch to check how it affects your payments: *6050 or 04-881-2345.

After a miscarriage (before 22 weeks)

  • One week of absence, paid from your sick days.
  • Longer if your doctor says so, up to 6 weeks.
  • Your employer may not dismiss you or reduce your job or pay during this absence without a permit from the Ministry of Labor.

Ask your employer or your workers’ committee what your contract or collective agreement adds. Some give more than the legal minimum.

You do not have to do any of this. Whatever you choose is right for you.

When you go home

Coming home without your baby can be one of the hardest moments. There is no right way to grieve and no timetable.

  • Your body may behave as after any birth, including milk coming in, bleeding and pain. Seek care straight away if you have heavy bleeding, strong pain or a fever.
  • Partners grieve too, often quietly. Read our guide for partners and fathers.
  • Brothers and sisters notice when something is wrong. Simple, honest words usually help more than silence. Some hospitals give parents a book to help explain the loss to young children. A seed letter can be a quiet activity to do together.
  • Difficult days often return: the due date, anniversaries, holidays, other people’s pregnancies. This is normal.
  • 15 October is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Remembrance Day. Many families light a candle at 7 pm.

You may also find comfort in our guidance for the days after loss.

You can plant a flower for your baby in our memorial garden. It stays there, with your baby’s name.

Hillel Yaffe Medical Center tells parents that a midwife will call after discharge to see how you are. Your health fund (kupat holim) may offer psychological support: ask your family doctor.
